The quadratic formula explained

The quadratic formula is one of the most important tools in algebra. It gives the solutions to any quadratic equation directly from the coefficients:

x = −b ± √(b² − 4ac)2a

You can use it whenever a quadratic equation cannot be solved easily by factoring. It works for every quadratic equation — including ones with irrational or complex roots.

The discriminant decides everything

The expression under the square root, b² − 4ac, is called the discriminant. Its value tells you what kind of roots to expect before you even finish the calculation:

Discriminant (D) Nature of roots Example
D > 0 Two distinct real roots x² − 5x + 6 = 0 → x = 2, 3
D = 0 One real root (repeated) x² − 4x + 4 = 0 → x = 2
D < 0 Two complex conjugate roots x² + x + 1 = 0 → x = −½ ± i√3⁄2

Worked example: x² − 5x + 6 = 0

Here a = 1, b = −5, c = 6.

  1. Compute the discriminant: D = (−5)² − 4(1)(6) = 25 − 24 = 1
  2. Since D > 0, there are two distinct real roots.
  3. Substitute into the formula: x = (5 ± √1) ÷ 2
  4. Root 1: x = (5 + 1) ÷ 2 = 3
  5. Root 2: x = (5 − 1) ÷ 2 = 2

Factoring confirms this: x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 2)(x − 3), so the roots are indeed 2 and 3.

Real-world applications of quadratic equations

Quadratic equations are not just abstract algebra — they show up in many real situations:

  • Projectile motion: The height of a ball, rocket, or thrown object over time follows a quadratic equation. Solving it tells you when the object hits the ground or reaches its peak.
  • Area problems: Finding the dimensions of a rectangle with a given perimeter and area often leads to a quadratic equation.
  • Profit and revenue: In business, revenue and profit functions are often quadratic. Their vertex gives the price or quantity that maximises profit.
  • Physics: Equations for kinetic energy, gravitational potential, and motion under constant acceleration are quadratic in form.
  • Engineering: Parabolic shapes appear in suspension bridge cables, satellite dishes, car headlights, and architectural arches.
  • Accident reconstruction: Traffic investigators use quadratic equations to estimate vehicle speed from skid-mark length and road friction.

Tips for solving quadratic equations

  • Put the equation in standard form first. Rearrange everything so the equation reads ax² + bx + c = 0 before identifying a, b, and c. A missing term means its coefficient is 0.
  • Check for easy factoring. If the numbers are small integers, try factoring before reaching for the formula. x² − 5x + 6 factors as (x − 2)(x − 3) in seconds.
  • Compute the discriminant early. Calculating D = b² − 4ac first tells you what kind of roots to expect, so you know whether to write real numbers or include i.
  • Watch the signs. The most common mistakes come from mishandling negative numbers, especially when b is negative and you substitute −b.
  • Simplify the root. If √D is not a perfect square, simplify it by pulling out perfect-square factors. √12 becomes 2√3, for example.
  • Verify with Vieta's formulas. The sum of the roots should equal −b ÷ a and the product should equal c ÷ a. This is a quick sanity check on your answer.
  • Remember the vertex tells you the extreme value. If a > 0, the vertex is the minimum of the parabola; if a < 0, it's the maximum. This is essential for optimisation problems.

Frequently asked questions

A quadratic equation is a second-degree polynomial equation of the form ax² + bx + c = 0, where a, b, and c are constants and a ≠ 0. The highest power of the unknown x is 2.
The quadratic formula is x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ÷ 2a. It gives the roots of any quadratic equation ax² + bx + c = 0 directly from the coefficients.
The discriminant is D = b² − 4ac. It determines the nature of the roots: if D > 0, there are two distinct real roots; if D = 0, there is exactly one real root (a repeated root); if D < 0, there are two complex conjugate roots with no real solutions.
When b² − 4ac < 0, the square root of a negative number is not real, so the equation has no real solutions. Instead, it has two complex conjugate roots of the form (−b ± i√|D|) ÷ 2a, where i is the imaginary unit.
The vertex is the highest or lowest point on the parabola y = ax² + bx + c. Its x-coordinate is x = −b ÷ 2a, and its y-coordinate is found by substituting this x-value back into the equation. If a > 0, the vertex is the minimum; if a < 0, it is the maximum.
The axis of symmetry is the vertical line that passes through the vertex and divides the parabola into two mirror-image halves. Its equation is x = −b ÷ 2a.
