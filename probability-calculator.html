Probability concepts explained

Single event probability

The probability of a single event A is the number of favourable outcomes divided by the total number of equally likely outcomes. If you roll a fair six-sided die, the probability of rolling a 3 is 1 favourable outcome ÷ 6 total outcomes = 1/6 ≈ 0.1667.

Complement

The complement of event A, written A′ or Aᶜ, is the probability that A does not happen. It is calculated as 1 − P(A). If the probability of rain tomorrow is 0.3, the probability of no rain is 0.7. The sum of an event and its complement is always exactly 1.

Union (A ∪ B)

The union is the probability that event A or event B (or both) occurs. The formula P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) avoids double-counting the overlap. If A and B are mutually exclusive (they cannot both happen), P(A ∩ B) = 0 and the formula simplifies to P(A) + P(B).

Intersection (A ∩ B)

The intersection is the probability that both A and B occur. Using conditional probability, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B|A). If A and B are independent, P(B|A) = P(B), so P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B). For example, the probability of flipping heads twice in a row is 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25.

Conditional probability (A | B)

Conditional probability is the probability of A given that B has already occurred. P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B). It requires P(B) > 0. This concept is central to Bayesian statistics and machine learning.

Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem updates a prior probability with new evidence. P(A|B) = [P(B|A) × P(A)] ÷ P(B). A classic example: a medical test is 90% accurate (P(B|A) = 0.9), the disease affects 1% of people (P(A) = 0.01), and 5% of tests are positive (P(B) = 0.05). Then P(disease | positive test) = (0.9 × 0.01) ÷ 0.05 = 0.18 — just 18%.

Permutations (nPr)

A permutation counts the number of ways to arrange r items chosen from n items where order matters. nPr = n! ÷ (n − r)!. For example, the number of ways to arrange 3 books from a shelf of 10 is 10P3 = 10! ÷ 7! = 720.

Combinations (nCr)

A combination counts the number of ways to choose r items from n items where order does not matter. nCr = n! ÷ [r! × (n − r)!]. For example, the number of ways to choose 3 people from a group of 10 is 10C3 = 120.

Where probability is used

  • Statistics: Hypothesis testing, confidence intervals, and p-values all rely on probability.
  • Machine learning: Bayesian inference, classification, and probabilistic models.
  • Medicine: Interpreting diagnostic tests, estimating risk, and clinical trials.
  • Finance: Risk assessment, option pricing, and portfolio theory.
  • Gaming: Calculating odds in poker, dice games, and lottery draws.
  • Engineering: Reliability analysis and quality control.
  • Weather forecasting: Expressing the likelihood of rain, storms, and other events.
