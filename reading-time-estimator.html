How to calculate reading time of an article

The math is boringly simple. It's just division.

Reading time (minutes) = Total words ÷ Reading speed (WPM)

If you have a 1,200-word article and your audience reads at 240 words per minute, that's 1,200 ÷ 240 = 5 minutes. If they read at 200 WPM, it's 6 minutes. Every speed change moves the answer.

There's no "correct" reading speed that applies to everyone. A technical paper read by an engineer averages around 150 WPM. A blog post skimmed on a phone might as well have been read at 400. The average adult reading speed for non-technical English sits somewhere between 200 and 250 WPM — that's why the default in this free reading time calculator is 238.

You can run this calculation by hand, or you can let the tool do it. Both work. The tool is faster and won't get the arithmetic wrong.

How long does it take to read an article?

It depends on the length of the article and who's reading it. But here's a quick rule of thumb that covers most cases:

  • A short article (300–600 words) takes 1–3 minutes.
  • A medium article (700–1,500 words) takes 3–6 minutes.
  • A long article (1,500–3,000 words) takes 6–13 minutes.
  • A long-form piece (3,000+ words) takes 13 minutes or more.

These numbers assume average adult reading speed (around 238 WPM). If your article is technical, add 30–50% to those times. If it's light reading, subtract 20–30%. The article reading time calculator above lets you adjust the speed to whatever matches your actual audience, so you don't have to guess.

Reading time calculator for blog posts and essays

Blog posts and essays have very different reading profiles, and the same words-per-minute number doesn't fit both. A blog reading time calculator usually wants a fairly fast default — 250 to 300 WPM — because blog readers skim. An essay, especially an academic one, is read much more slowly. 150 to 200 WPM is a more honest estimate there.

If you're using this page as a reading time calculator for essays, drop the reading speed slider down before you paste. The estimates will adjust instantly. If you're calculating reading time for a blog post, leave it near the default or push it slightly higher.

How long will it take to read my text?

The fastest way to answer that is to paste it in. This online text reading time estimator gives you the answer in under a second.

But if you want a rough mental model first: a full page of A4 with standard formatting (1-inch margins, 12pt font, single spacing) usually holds around 500 words. So:

  • 1 page ≈ 2 minutes
  • 2 pages ≈ 4 minutes
  • 5 pages ≈ 10 minutes
  • 10 pages ≈ 21 minutes

Those are rough figures for standard prose. Dialogue, poetry, code, and short-line formats all read very differently and don't fit the page-based estimate. For those, paste the actual text and use the tool's real word count.

Speaking time vs. reading time

These two numbers are often confused, and they're genuinely different things.

Reading time is silent. The eyes move across the page; the mouth doesn't move. Average silent reading speed sits around 238 WPM.

Speaking time is slower. The mouth has to physically shape every word. Presentations, speeches, and video scripts run closer to 130–150 WPM. Podcast hosts and audiobook narrators average around 150. Fast talkers can hit 180–200, but most audiences find that pace hard to follow.

This tool estimates both because both matter. If you're writing a blog post, you care about reading time. If you're writing a presentation script, you care about speaking time. Sometimes you care about both, and that's why the results panel shows two numbers side by side. There's a dedicated speaking time calculator if you only need the speech estimate.

Tips for content creators

A few practical things worth knowing once you have reading time in front of you:

  • Display reading time at the top of long articles. Readers appreciate the transparency, and posts that do this tend to keep people around longer.
  • Break up reads over 10 minutes. Add subheadings, section dividers, or split the piece into a series.
  • Match the format to the speed. Mobile readers skim faster than desktop readers. If most of your traffic is on phones, assume a faster average WPM.
  • Test both speeds. Show your content to a slow reader and a fast reader and see how the estimate shifts. If the range is wide, you may want to give a range on the page instead of a single number.
  • Use the tool for speaking time before recording. If your video script reads as 12 minutes at 130 WPM, that's your actual runtime. No surprises in the edit.
