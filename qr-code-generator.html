What is a QR code?

A QR (Quick Response) code is a two-dimensional barcode that stores data in a grid of black and white squares. Created in 1994 by Denso Wave for tracking car parts, it has since become the universal way to move a URL, contact card, Wi-Fi password, or short message from a screen into a phone with a single scan.

Unlike a traditional 1D barcode, a QR code can hold up to ~2,900 characters and can still be read when part of it is damaged or covered — that's what the error correction level controls. Modern phone cameras scan QR codes natively, so no third-party app is needed.

Every QR code on this page is static: the data is encoded directly into the image, so there is no server, no redirect, and no expiry. Save the PNG or SVG and it will keep working forever, even if you take this page offline.

How to generate a QR code

  1. Pick a content type. Choose Text, URL, Email, Phone, Wi-Fi, or Contact (vCard) from the dropdown. The form fields change to match the type you pick.
  2. Fill in the details. For a URL, paste the full link (https://…). For Wi-Fi, enter the network name, security type, and password so guests can join with one scan.
  3. Choose your options. Set the output size in pixels, choose an error-correction level (M is a good default), and keep the margin at 4 modules unless you have a specific reason not to.
  4. Adjust colors if you need to. Dark foreground on a light, opaque background gives the best scannability. Avoid transparent backgrounds — many scanners refuse to read them.
  5. Preview live. The QR code updates as you type. If the code looks dense or the preview doesn't appear, reduce the data length or lower the error correction to L.
  6. Download or copy. Grab a PNG for immediate use, or an SVG for print and design work. Click Copy to put the image on your clipboard.

Error correction levels explained

A QR code can still be scanned when part of it is missing or damaged. The error correction level controls how much of the code can be lost and still be recovered. Higher levels make the QR code denser (more modules for the same data), so you trade scan resilience for visual size.

LevelRecovery capacityMax byte capacityBest for
L (low)Up to 7% damage~2,953 bytesLarge amounts of data, ideal conditions
M (medium)Up to 15% damage~2,331 bytesGeneral use — the default for most QR codes
Q (quartile)Up to 25% damage~1,663 bytesIndustrial use, harsh environments
H (high)Up to 30% damage~1,273 bytesCodes with a logo or image overlaid in the center

If you plan to put your brand logo in the middle of the QR code, choose H — the extra redundancy means the code still scans even with the central 30% obscured.

Tips for a scannable QR code

  • Keep the margin. QR codes need a quiet zone around them (usually 4 modules). If the margin is too small, scanners fail to lock on.
  • Maximize contrast. Dark foreground on a light background is the safest. Very light foregrounds on dark backgrounds can work, but only if the scanner supports inverted codes — many don't.
  • Don't use transparency. A transparent background may look clean on your design, but most phone cameras will refuse to scan it. Always use an opaque background.
  • Keep it big enough. A minimum of 2 cm × 2 cm (about 0.8 in) on print is a good starting point — larger for dense codes or long URLs.
  • Test before printing. Scan your QR code with at least two different phones before committing to a print run or embedding it in a physical product.
  • Shorten long URLs. Every extra character makes the QR code denser. If your URL is long, shorten it first to keep the code visually clean and easy to scan.

If your QR code stops scanning at a smaller size, the problem is usually data density, not the image resolution. Reduce the amount of data (shorten the URL, drop optional fields in the vCard, use error correction M instead of H) and regenerate — the code will become larger per module and much easier to scan.

Common use cases

Marketing and print

Drop a QR code on a poster, business card, product label, or menu so people can jump straight to a landing page, menu, or promo without typing a URL. SVG export is ideal here — it stays sharp at any print size.

Wi-Fi sharing

Generate a Wi-Fi QR code for guests, cafés, or Airbnbs. Visitors scan once and join the network automatically — no more dictating 16-character passwords.

Contactless contact sharing

Encode a vCard with your name, title, company, phone, email, and website. When scanned, most phones offer to save the contact directly to their address book.

Event check-in and tickets

Give each attendee a unique QR code with their ticket ID. Scanning at the door is faster and more reliable than checking names against a list.

Digital menus and payments

Restaurants, cafés, and market stalls use QR codes to link to digital menus or UPI / payment apps. Static codes mean there is nothing to maintain once printed.

Education and handouts

Add a QR code to a worksheet or slide deck that links to a video, reading, or extra practice set. Students scan with the camera app — no app install needed.
