What does this Word Frequency Counter do?

A word frequency counter analyses a piece of text and tells you how often each distinct word appears. Instead of just giving you a single number, it builds a ranked table: the most-used words at the top, each with its exact count and what percentage of the total text it represents. It's the quickest way to see what a document is actually "about" at the vocabulary level, and to spot repetition you might not notice by eye.

This tool does the counting entirely in your browser. The text is tokenised by splitting on whitespace, stripping punctuation and symbols from the edges of each token, and treating hyphenated words like well-known as a single term. You can then shape the results with a few controls: ignore case so that "The" and "the" count together, exclude stop words so that common filler doesn't dominate the list, require a minimum word length to filter out short fragments, and choose how the results are sorted and how many to show.

Everything updates live as you type, with no button to press and no server round-trip. The text never leaves your device.

How to use the Word Frequency Counter

  1. Paste or type your text into the textarea on the left. You can paste from any source — a document, a webpage, an email, or a code file.
  2. Watch the frequency table populate instantly in the panel on the right. There's no "analyse" button; every option recalculates the table as you change it.
  3. Adjust the options to shape the analysis. Turn off "Ignore case" if capitalisation matters. Turn off "Exclude stop words" if you want to see everything, including common filler words.
  4. Set a minimum word length to filter out short tokens like "a", "an", "is", and "to".
  5. Choose a sort order and how many rows to display.
  6. Export the results with "Copy as CSV" or "Copy Summary".

How to interpret the results

The frequency table is simple on the surface — word, count, percentage — but the patterns it reveals are what makes it useful. Here's what to look for.

1. A single word dominating the list

If one word appears far more often than anything else, that's the dominant term. In a topical blog post, that's usually the target keyword and it's expected. In a general essay, it might be a sign that you've leaned on the same word too hard. If your target keyword appears at more than about 3–4% frequency, that's usually a sign of over-optimisation for SEO.

2. A long tail of one-hit wonders

Most natural text has a handful of very frequent words and a long tail of words that appear only once. If nearly all words in your text appear only once, the vocabulary is unusually rich — or the text is very short.

3. Stop words dominating

If you turn off "Exclude stop words", the top of the list will almost always be the same: the, of, and, to, a, in, is. That's normal English. It's exactly why the filter exists.

4. Proper nouns

A capitalised word near the top of the list often reveals the subject matter: a person, place, product, or brand. When "Ignore case" is on, this signal is lost — which is why the option to turn it off exists.

5. Words that should be there but aren't

Sometimes the most useful insight is what's missing. If you're writing about a specific topic and its main term doesn't appear in the top 20, that's a signal that the text isn't focused on what you think it is.

What are stop words?

Stop words are the highest-frequency words in a language — the grammatical glue that holds sentences together. In English they include articles (a, an, the), conjunctions (and, or, but), prepositions (of, in, to, for), pronouns (I, you, it, they), and common verbs (is, are, was, have).

In any natural text, these words occupy the top of a raw frequency list — which is precisely why they're not useful for analysis. Filtering them out reveals the words that actually carry meaning.

This tool ships with a built-in stop-word list of over 100 common English words. You can toggle the filter on or off with a single click.
