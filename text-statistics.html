What does this Text Statistics tool do?

This free online text statistics tool analyzes any body of text and gives you a complete numerical breakdown — instantly. Paste an essay, a blog post, a report, a speech, or a social media caption, and the tool calculates dozens of metrics covering length, structure, readability, and word usage.

The metrics fall into five main categories: counts (words, characters, sentences, paragraphs, lines), averages (average word length, average sentence length, syllables), time estimates (reading time, speaking time), readability scores (Flesch Reading Ease, Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, Gunning Fog Index, Automated Readability Index), and keyword frequency (top words used in the text).

Everything runs locally in your browser — the text you enter never leaves your device. No signup, no server uploads, no limits. The statistics update live as you type, so you can see the impact of every edit in real time.

How to use the Text Statistics tool

  1. Paste or type your text into the input box on the left. You can paste from any source — a document, a website, an email, or just start typing.
  2. Watch the statistics update instantly in the panel on the right. There's no "calculate" button — every metric recalculates as you type or paste.
  3. Review the four highlight cards at the top for the most important numbers: words, characters, sentences, and paragraphs.
  4. Scroll through the detailed sections for counts, averages, time estimates, readability scores, and your top keywords.
  5. Copy the stats with the "Copy Stats" button if you want to save or share the analysis. The "Clear Text" button resets everything.

That's it. No signup, no email, no waiting — just paste and read.

Readability formulas explained

Readability scores estimate how easy or difficult a text is to read. They're widely used by educators, publishers, and content marketers to match text to the right audience. This tool calculates four of the most well-known formulas.

Flesch Reading Ease (0–100, higher = easier)
206.835 − (1.015 × average sentence length) − (84.6 × average syllables per word)
Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (U.S. school grade)
0.39 × (total words ÷ total sentences) + 11.8 × (total syllables ÷ total words) − 15.59
Gunning Fog Index (years of education)
0.4 × [(words ÷ sentences) + 100 × (complex words ÷ words)]
Automated Readability Index (U.S. grade level)
4.71 × (characters ÷ words) + 0.5 × (words ÷ sentences) − 21.43

How to interpret the scores

  • Flesch Reading Ease: 90–100 = very easy (5th grade), 60–70 = standard (8th–9th grade), 30–50 = difficult (college level), 0–30 = very difficult (graduate level).
  • Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level: A score of 8 means an eighth-grader can understand it. Most web content targets grades 6–9.
  • Gunning Fog Index: A score of 12 means a high-school senior can read it. Business writing typically targets 10–12; technical writing is often 14+.
  • Automated Readability Index: Similar to Flesch-Kincaid — it returns a U.S. grade level. Lower is easier.

How reading time and speaking time are calculated

Reading time is estimated using the average adult silent reading speed of 225 words per minute. Speaking time uses a slower rate of 150 words per minute, which is typical for clear, measured speech.

These are estimates, not guarantees. Actual reading speed varies with text complexity, reader familiarity with the topic, and device. Speaking speed varies with delivery style, pauses, and audience. But for most practical purposes — blog posts, speeches, presentations, scripts — these estimates are accurate within a reasonable margin.

Frequently asked questions

Text statistics is the process of analysing a body of text to extract quantitative metrics such as word count, character count, sentence count, paragraph count, average word length, reading time, speaking time, and readability scores. These metrics help writers, editors, students, and marketers understand the structure, complexity, and readability of their content.
The Flesch Reading Ease score is a readability test that rates text on a 0–100 scale. Higher scores indicate easier reading. A score of 60–70 is considered standard, 70–80 is fairly easy, and 90–100 is very easy. The formula uses average sentence length and average syllables per word.
The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level estimates the U.S. school grade level required to understand a text. For example, a score of 8 means an eighth-grader can read it. It uses average sentence length and average syllables per word.
The Gunning Fog Index estimates the years of formal education required to understand a text. It considers average sentence length and the percentage of complex words (words with three or more syllables). A lower score means easier reading.
Reading time is estimated by dividing the total word count by the average adult reading speed, typically 200–250 words per minute. This tool uses 225 words per minute for reading time and 150 words per minute for speaking time.
Syllables are estimated using a rule-based algorithm that counts vowel groups and adjusts for common English patterns (silent e, diphthongs, etc.). It's accurate enough for readability scoring and time estimates, though no automated syllable counter is perfect for every English word.
