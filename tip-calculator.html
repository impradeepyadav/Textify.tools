What does this Tip Calculator do?

This free online tip calculator works out the tip amount, the total bill (including tip), and exactly how much each person owes when you split the bill. Just enter the bill amount, pick or type a tip percentage, choose how many people are splitting, and everything updates instantly.

It's perfect for restaurants, cafés, taxis, delivery drivers, and any situation where you want to tip and split fairly. You can also round up the per-person amount. Choose from 40+ currencies including USD, EUR, GBP, INR, JPY, AUD, CAD, and more.

And when you want to add a bit of fun, use the "Who Pays the Bill?" spin wheel below to randomly pick who picks up the check — perfect for group dinners, game nights, or friendly get-togethers.

How to use the tip calculator

  1. Enter the bill amount in the box at the top.
  2. Choose your currency from the dropdown next to the bill amount.

How much to tip in different situations

Beyond restaurants, there are plenty of everyday services where a tip is appreciated. Here's a practical guide for common tipping situations.

Food and drink

  • Restaurants (table service): 15–20% of the pre-tax bill in the US, 10–15% in most of Europe. Check for a service charge first.
  • Cafés and coffee shops: $1–$2 in the tip jar, or round up to the nearest dollar.
  • Food delivery: 10–15% of the order total, minimum $3–$5 depending on distance and weather.
  • Takeout: Not required, but $1–$2 is a nice gesture for large or complex orders.
  • Bartenders: $1–$2 per drink, or 15–20% on a tab.

Transportation

  • Taxi / rideshare: 10–15% of the fare, or round up to the nearest note. Rideshares often include a tip option in the app.
  • Airport shuttle: $2–$5 per person, more if the driver helps with luggage.
  • Valet parking: $2–$5 when you pick up the car.
  • Hotel shuttle: $1–$2 per bag.

Hotels

  • Housekeeping: $2–$5 per night, left daily (staff may rotate).
  • Bellhop / porter: $1–$2 per bag.
  • Concierge: $5–$20 for special requests like hard-to-get reservations.
  • Room service: 15–20% if not already added to the bill.

Personal services

  • Hair stylist / barber: 15–20% of the service cost.
  • Nail salon: 15–20%.
  • Massage therapist: 15–20%.
  • Tattoo artist: 15–20%, especially for custom work.
  • Pet groomer: 15–20%.

Other services

  • Movers: $20–$50 per mover for a full day, plus water and snacks.
  • Furniture delivery: $5–$20 per person, depending on difficulty.
  • Home services (plumber, electrician): Not required for a service call, but appreciated for exceptional work.

Understanding tip percentages (with examples)

Tip percentages are easy once you know the trick. Here are real examples using common bill amounts so you can see exactly how the math works.

Example 1: A $60 restaurant bill with a 20% tip

  • Tip = $60 × 0.20 = $12.00
  • Total = $60 + $12 = $72.00
  • Split between 2 people = $72 ÷ 2 = $36.00 each

Example 2: A ₹2,500 dinner bill with a 10% tip, split 5 ways

  • Tip = ₹2,500 × 0.10 = ₹250
  • Total = ₹2,500 + ₹250 = ₹2,750
  • Per person = ₹2,750 ÷ 5 = ₹550 each

Example 3: A £45 taxi fare with a rounded-up tip

  • Suggested tip = £45 × 0.15 = £6.75
  • Rounded up per-person total = £52.00

Quick mental math shortcuts

  • 10% tip: Move the decimal point one place to the left. $80 → $8.
  • 15% tip: Calculate 10%, then add half of it. $80 → $8 + $4 = $12.
  • 20% tip: Calculate 10%, then double it. $80 → $8 × 2 = $16.
  • 25% tip: Calculate 10%, double it, then add half of the 10%. $80 → $16 + $4 = $20.

Of course, this calculator does all that instantly — but it's handy to know the shortcuts when you're out without your phone.

How bill splitting works (and how to do it fairly)

Splitting a bill sounds simple, but it's rarely straightforward when people ordered different things. Here are the three most common ways to split, and when each one makes sense.

1. Split evenly

The total (including tip) is divided by the number of people. Everyone pays the same amount.

  • Best for: Groups where everyone ordered similar amounts, or when nobody wants to do math at the table.
  • Downside: Someone who ordered a salad ends up subsidising the person who ordered steak and three cocktails.

2. Split by what each person ordered

Each person pays for their own items, plus a proportional share of the tip and any shared items (like appetisers).

  • Best for: Larger groups with very different orders.
  • Downside: Takes longer to calculate, especially with shared plates.

3. Hybrid split

Shared items (appetisers, bottles of wine, dessert) are split evenly between everyone. Individual items are paid by whoever ordered them. The tip is then split proportionally.

  • Best for: Most real-world group dinners.
  • Downside: Requires a bit more effort to track.

Rounding up

Many people prefer to round the per-person amount up to the nearest whole number. This avoids awkward change and often leaves a slightly bigger tip, which servers appreciate. Toggle "Round up the per-person amount" in the calculator to enable this.

The Who Pays the Bill spin wheel is a fun, fair way to settle who picks up the check. It's included on this page as an optional feature, right below the tip calculator.

Tipping etiquette tips (the unwritten rules)

Tipping isn't just about the amount — it's also about how you do it. A few things that experienced diners and travellers know:

  • Check the bill for service charge. In many countries, a 10–15% service charge is already added. If it is, you don't need to add another full tip — a small extra is fine for great service.
  • Tip on the pre-tax amount, not the total. Purists argue tips should be calculated before tax is added. In practice, most people tip on the total.
  • Cash tips are still appreciated. Even in a card-heavy world, cash goes directly to staff without processing fees.
  • Don't leave coins as an insult. Small change is fine in some countries, but in places like Germany or Japan, leaving a few coins can come across as dismissive.
  • Reward good service, not bad service. If service was genuinely poor, a lower tip (or none) is a legitimate signal. If it was excellent, a slightly higher tip is a nice touch.
  • Consider the situation. A busy Friday night, extreme weather, or a large group all mean more work for the staff — a slightly bigger tip reflects that.
  • Don't tip in a way that embarrasses. In some cultures, tipping is unusual. A quiet, discreet tip is always better than a loud one.
  • Use the wheel for group decisions. If your group is arguing about who pays, the Who Pays the Bill wheel settles it in seconds — fairly and without hurt feelings.
