What does this NATO Alphabet Generator do?


This free online NATO Alphabet Generator converts plain text into the NATO phonetic alphabet — the internationally recognised spelling system used in aviation, military, and maritime communication. Type a message and get the code words instantly (A becomes Alfa, B becomes Bravo, and so on), or paste NATO code words and read the original text back.


The generator supports the full 26-letter alphabet plus digits 0–9 with their modified ICAO pronunciations: Tree for three, Fower for four, Fife for five, and Niner for nine. These modified pronunciations exist to keep each digit distinct when spoken over noisy radio channels — Tree avoids confusion with "three" said quickly, Fife avoids the "v" sound that can sound like "fire", and Niner has an extra syllable to keep it distinct from the German word for "no".


Everything runs locally. The text you enter never leaves your device, there is no signup, and there are no usage limits. Convert as much as you want, as many times as you want, for personal or commercial projects.

Tips for using the NATO phonetic alphabet


    

  • Say each word clearly with a brief pause. The whole point of the alphabet is that each word stands on its own. Don't rush them together.
    • 

  • Use the ICAO spellings. Write Alfa, Juliett, and Xray — not Alpha, Juliet, or X-ray. The alternate spellings exist so non-English speakers pronounce them correctly.
    • 

  • Numbers are spelled digit by digit. "17" becomes One-Seven, not Seventeen. This avoids ambiguity about where one number ends and the next begins.
    • 

  • Use word breaks in long strings. When converting a sentence, turn on Add word breaks between words so each word's code words stay grouped together — easier to read back aloud.
    • 

  • Watch out for the "two/to/too" problem. The NATO alphabet has Two for the digit 2, but it doesn't distinguish those English homophones — context always decides.
    • 

  • Some regions use variants. British police use "Indigo" instead of India; some US airports use "Dixie" instead of Delta. These are local substitutions, not part of the standard ICAO alphabet.

Frequently asked questions



The NATO phonetic alphabet — officially the International Radiotelephony Spelling Alphabet — is a set of code words assigned to each letter of the Latin alphabet and to the digits 0–9. It was defined in 1955–1956 by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) to reduce errors in spoken communication over radio and telephone. A is Alfa, B is Bravo, C is Charlie, and so on through Z is Zulu.



The spelling Alfa (with an f) is used deliberately to ensure speakers of languages that don't naturally pronounce the "ph" combination as "f" still say it correctly. Similarly, Juliett has a double-t to help French speakers avoid treating the final t as silent, and Xray is written as one word (rather than X-ray) so it's pronounced as a single unit.



Digits are spelled out individually, using the ICAO pronunciations: Zero, One, Two, Tree, Fower (for Four), Fife (for Five), Six, Seven, Eight, Niner (for Nine). The modified pronunciations keep digits distinct over noisy radio channels — Tree avoids confusion with "three" said quickly, Fife avoids the "v" sound that can sound like "fire", and Niner adds a syllable to keep it distinct from the German word "nein".



The NATO alphabet is used wherever spoken clarity matters: aviation and air traffic control, military communications, maritime and emergency services, amateur radio, and increasingly by IT support, call centres, and retail for spelling out names, addresses, serial numbers, email addresses, and reference codes over the phone.
