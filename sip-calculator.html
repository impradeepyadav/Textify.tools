What is a SIP?

A SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) is a disciplined way of investing a fixed amount at regular intervals — usually every month — into a mutual fund, index fund, or similar financial instrument. Instead of investing a large lump sum at one point, you spread your investment over many months or years.

SIPs are popular because they remove emotion from investing. You commit to a fixed monthly amount, and the money flows automatically. Over time, this habit compounds into significant wealth. This calculator helps you see what those future numbers could look like before you commit.

Although SIPs are commonly associated with Indian mutual funds, the concept is universal. Investors in the US, Europe, and elsewhere use the same idea under names like "automatic investment plans" or "dollar-cost averaging." The math is identical — only the currency and naming differ.

How the SIP calculator works

The calculator uses the standard future value formula for a series of equal monthly investments earning compound returns:

FV = P × [((1 + i)n − 1) ÷ i] × (1 + i)
  • P — Monthly investment amount.
  • i — Monthly rate of return. If the annual return is 12%, then i = 12 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.01.
  • n — Total number of monthly installments. A 10-year SIP has n = 120.

The extra (1 + i) at the end assumes each monthly contribution is made at the beginning of the month (the standard SIP convention), giving the money one extra month to grow.

Worked example

Suppose you invest $500 per month for 10 years at an expected return of 12% per year:

  • Monthly rate i = 0.01
  • n = 120 months
  • Total invested = $500 × 120 = $60,000
  • FV = 500 × [((1.01)120 − 1) ÷ 0.01] × 1.01 ≈ $115,019
  • Estimated returns ≈ $55,019
  • Wealth multiple ≈ 1.92×

Enter these values in the calculator above and you'll see the same figures. That's the power of compounding — you invest $60,000, and it grows to nearly $115,000.

Why SIPs work: the two superpowers

1. Compounding

Returns are reinvested and earn their own returns. Over 10–20 years, compounding becomes the dominant driver of your final corpus — often generating more than your total contributions.

2. Rupee/currency cost averaging

You invest the same amount every month. When markets fall, your money buys more units; when they rise, fewer. Over time, this averages out your purchase price and reduces the risk of bad timing.

These two forces work together. Compounding rewards time; cost averaging rewards consistency. Neither requires you to be a market expert.

Past performance doesn't guarantee future results. Use 10–12% as a reasonable equity assumption, 6–8% for balanced, and 3–5% for debt. Smaller assumptions give you a more conservative picture.

SIP vs lump sum: which is better?

Both approaches have merits. The right choice depends on your situation:

  • SIP is better if: You earn a regular salary, want to avoid the risk of investing at a market peak, or find it hard to save a large amount at once.
  • Lump sum is better if: You have a windfall (bonus, inheritance), the market is clearly undervalued, and your investment horizon is long.

In most cases, SIP wins for salaried investors because it enforces discipline. But if you receive a large one-time amount and don't need it for years, investing it in phases (called STP — Systematic Transfer Plan) combines the benefits of both.

8 tips for SIP success

  1. Start early. Every year you delay costs you years of compounding. Even small amounts invested at 25 beat larger amounts invested at 35.
  2. Increase your SIP annually. A 10% annual step-up can nearly double your final corpus over 20 years.
  3. Don't stop in market crashes. Downturns are when your SIP buys more units — that's the point of cost averaging.
  4. Choose the right fund. Low-cost index funds beat most actively managed funds over the long run.
  5. Keep the horizon long. Minimum 7–10 years for equity SIPs to smooth out volatility.
  6. Review annually, not weekly. Checking daily leads to panic selling. Once a year is enough.
  7. Use step-up SIPs after raises. Every salary increase is a chance to increase your SIP proportionally.
  8. Have an emergency fund first. Don't invest money you might need in the next 12 months — that's what savings accounts are for.

The step-up SIP advantage

A step-up SIP (or top-up SIP) increases your monthly amount by a fixed percentage every year. It's one of the most powerful wealth-building strategies available. For example:

  • Regular SIP: $500/month for 20 years at 12% → $494,278
  • Step-up SIP with 10% annual increase: $500/month starting, up 10% each year at 12% → ~$1,050,000

That's more than double the corpus — simply by scaling contributions with your income. Most fund platforms support this automatically.

Common SIP mistakes to avoid

  • Stopping during downturns. This is the biggest mistake. Bear markets are when SIPs shine.
  • Chasing last year's best fund. Past performance rarely repeats. Choose based on cost, category, and consistency.
  • Redeeming too early. Equity SIPs need a 7–10 year horizon minimum to deliver their long-term averages.
  • Ignoring fees. A 1% higher expense ratio can cost you 15–20% of your final corpus over 25 years.
  • Investing emergency money. Keep 3–6 months of expenses in liquid savings, not in equity SIPs.
  • Not tracking inflation. A 12% nominal return is only ~9% real return after 3% inflation. Plan accordingly.
