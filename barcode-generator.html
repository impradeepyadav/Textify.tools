What is a barcode?

A barcode is a machine-readable pattern of parallel bars and spaces of varying widths. When a scanner passes over it, the pattern is decoded into a string of characters — a product number, a tracking code, a patient ID, or any other identifier. Unlike QR codes, which are two-dimensional, traditional barcodes store data in one dimension and are still the standard for retail checkout, warehouse logistics, shipping labels, and library systems.

The format you choose matters. CODE128 can encode any printable ASCII character and is the most versatile general-purpose format. EAN-13 and UPC-A are the fixed-length retail formats you see on products sold in shops. CODE39 is an older alphanumeric format that is still widely used in industrial and government applications. ITF-14 is used on shipping cartons. Each format has its own rules for length, allowed characters, and check digits.

Every barcode on this page is static: the data is encoded directly into the image, so there is no server, no redirect, and no expiry. Save the PNG or SVG and it will keep working forever, even if you take this page offline.

How to generate a barcode

  1. Pick a format. Choose CODE128 for general-purpose use, EAN-13 or UPC-A for retail products, CODE39 for alphanumeric industrial labels, or any other format that fits your needs.
  2. Enter a value. Type the data you want to encode. The tool validates it against the format you selected and tells you if something is wrong (for example, an EAN-13 value must be 13 digits and have a valid check digit).
  3. Adjust the appearance. Set the bar width, bar height, quiet zone, and label size. Choose bar and background colors — dark bars on a light opaque background scan most reliably.
  4. Preview live. The barcode updates as you type. If the preview does not appear, check the status line for a validation message.
  5. Download or copy. Grab a PNG for immediate use, or an SVG for print and design work. Click Copy to put the image on your clipboard.

Tips for a scannable barcode

  • Keep the quiet zone. Barcodes need blank space on the left and right — usually at least 10 times the width of the narrowest bar. If the quiet zone is too small, scanners fail to detect the start and end of the barcode.
  • Maximize contrast. Dark bars on a light background is the safest combination. Very light bars on a dark background can work, but only if the scanner supports inverted codes — many don't.
  • Don't use transparency. A transparent background may look clean on your design, but most scanners will refuse to read it. Always use an opaque background.
  • Match the bar width to the print size. If you're printing small, use a bar width of 1–2 px. For larger labels, 2–3 px gives a cleaner result. Test with a real scanner before a print run.
  • Use the right format. If you control both ends, CODE128 is the most reliable and versatile choice. If the barcode must be read by retail POS systems, use the format the retailer requires (EAN-13 or UPC-A).
  • Validate before printing. EAN-13, EAN-8, UPC-A, and ITF-14 all have specific length and check-digit rules. The tool validates your input and tells you if something is wrong.

If your barcode stops scanning at a smaller size, the problem is usually bar width or quiet zone, not the image resolution. Increase the bar width, keep the quiet zone at 10 px or more, and test with a real scanner before committing to a print run.

Common use cases

Retail and product labeling

Use EAN-13 or UPC-A for products sold in shops. These are the formats that retail POS systems expect, and they include a check digit so scanners can detect read errors.

Shipping and logistics

Use CODE128 or ITF-14 on shipping labels and cartons. CODE128 is flexible enough to encode tracking numbers, while ITF-14 is the standard for corrugated shipping boxes.

Inventory and asset tracking

Use CODE128 or CODE39 on shelves, bins, and assets. A short, stable identifier is all you need — the barcode just has to be readable at a distance and survive a warehouse environment.

Pharmaceutical packaging

Use Pharmacode for small pharmaceutical packages where a compact, numeric-only barcode is required. The format is designed to be printed at very small sizes.

Libraries and blood banks

Use Codabar for patron cards, book spines, and blood bag labels. Codabar is an older format, but it is still widely used in these industries because existing scanners already support it.

Event ticketing and access control

Use CODE128 to encode a unique ticket ID on each pass. The barcode can be scanned at the door and matched against a list in real time.
