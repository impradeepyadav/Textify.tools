What does this UUID validator do?

A UUID validator checks whether a string is a syntactically valid universally unique identifier and then explains what it contains. The bare minimum check is format: a UUID is 128 bits, conventionally written as 32 hexadecimal digits in five hyphenated groups. But format alone isn't enough to call a UUID "valid" — the version nibble and variant bits also need to fall within the values defined by the spec.

This tool goes further. For every UUID it parses, it reports the version (v1 through v8) and variant, extracts the embedded timestamp from time-based versions (v1, v6, v7), shows the MAC address and clock sequence for v1 UUIDs, and normalizes the value into every common representation — canonical, uppercase, compact, braces, and URN. If you paste a batch, you get a summary of how many were valid.

Everything runs locally in your browser. The UUIDs you paste never leave your device, there's no signup, and there are no usage limits.

UUID structure, bit by bit

A UUID is a 128-bit value. When written in canonical form xxxxxxxx-xxxx-Mxxx-Nxxx-xxxxxxxxxxxx, the hyphens are purely cosmetic — the 32 hex digits are what matter. The two nibbles shown here as M and N are the version and the first nibble of the variant field.

ByteBitsMeaning
0–3time_lowLeast significant 32 bits of the timestamp (v1) or the random data (v4).
4–5time_midNext 16 bits of the timestamp, or random data.
6versionHigh nibble is the version number 1–8. The low nibble is part of the time_high field (v1) or random data.
7time_highRemaining 8 bits of the 12-bit time_high field.
8variantHigh 2 bits identify the variant family. The remaining bits are the clock sequence (v1) or random data.
9—Clock sequence or random data.
10–15node48-bit node identifier — usually a MAC address (v1), or random data.

UUID versions and what they mean

VersionNameWhat it's built from
1Time-based60-bit timestamp since 1582-10-15 + 14-bit clock sequence + 48-bit node (usually a MAC address).
2DCE securityLike v1 but with a POSIX UID/GID in place of the time_low field. Almost never used outside legacy DCE.
3MD5 name-basedMD5 hash of a namespace UUID and a name. Deterministic: same inputs → same UUID.
4Random122 bits of cryptographic randomness. The most common version in modern applications.
5SHA-1 name-basedSHA-1 hash of a namespace UUID and a name. Deterministic and preferred over v3.
6Reordered timeSame data as v1 but with the timestamp fields reordered so the UUID sorts by time.
7Unix epoch time48-bit Unix millisecond timestamp + 74 random bits. Sortable, and the modern choice for database keys.
8CustomApplication-defined. The version field is 8 but the remaining 122 bits follow whatever scheme the app chooses.

Versions 9 through 15 are reserved for future definition. A UUID that reports one of those version numbers is either produced by an extension or by a buggy generator.

What makes a UUID valid or invalid

There are three levels of "valid" for a UUID, and this tool lets you decide how strict you want to be:

  • Syntactic validity. The string has 32 hex characters (with or without hyphens, braces, or a URN prefix), nothing else. Any string that doesn't parse into exactly 128 bits is invalid.
  • Version validity. The version nibble is 1 through 8. Version 0 is only valid for the nil UUID (all zeros). Versions 9–15 are reserved and technically invalid.
  • Variant validity. The high two bits of byte 8 follow the RFC 4122 pattern (10xx). The NCS, Microsoft, and Future variants use different bit patterns and are unusual to see in modern systems.

When Strict RFC validation is on, both version and variant must match. When it's off, only syntax is required — which matches what most parsers actually enforce. Many UUID generators, including some popular libraries, will happily emit UUIDs with non-RFC variant bits or non-standard versions, and most applications accept them without complaint.
